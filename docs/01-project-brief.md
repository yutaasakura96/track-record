# 01 — Project Brief

**Status:** Phase 1 · written 2026-08-11 · supersedes `00-kickoff.md`

---

## In one sentence

**Track Record turns a career's worth of long-form work documentation into a structured, provenance-tracked record of facts, and generates every document that career needs — English résumé, 履歴書, 職務経歴書, and a bilingual pair of interview stories — from that record.**

## The problem, and whose it is

Today the author maintains a hand-written corpus of long technical case studies, one per project, generated by running a standing prompt inside each work repository. Renders are produced by prompting an assistant to read that entire corpus and write a document.

The loop's **shape** is right. Its **cost** is in the wrong place: every render re-reads the whole corpus and re-extracts the same facts from scratch. Three consequences, all currently being lived with:

- Updates are slow, so they get batched instead of happening per achievement.
- Output is not repeatable — the same request twice produces two different résumés.
- Updates are wholesale rewrites rather than reviewable diffs, so prior hand-tuning is lost each pass.

**The expensive part is re-reading, not writing.** A structured record layer between the evidence and the renders removes it: facts are extracted once, each pointing back at the passage that proves it, and renders read the facts instead of the corpus.

**First real user:** the author. Also the only user for the foreseeable future.

## What kind of project this is

A **serious side project** — not a weekend hack, not a product today. It is intended to become the author's permanent system of record for their career, and to grow over years.

Built for one user, but **no schema decision may assume exactly one person exists**. No multi-tenancy, roles, or settings surfaces now. This line is what keeps speculative complexity out while leaving the product option open. If it ever becomes a product, the wedge is **bilingual**: very little on the market handles 履歴書 and 職務経歴書 properly alongside Western résumés.

## What success looks like

There is no deadline. Success is defined by milestones, in order, and by one behavioural test.

**M1 — Switchable for one document.** One case study imported for one employer. Facts extracted with provenance and disclosure level set. The English résumé generates from those facts alone, with every bullet traceable back to the passage that proves it. Explicitly **not** in M1: 履歴書, 職務経歴書, the career stories, or any other employer.

**M2 — All five renders.** Every output generating, every change landing through a diff-and-accept review gate, 履歴書 conventionally correct.

**M3 — The old workflow is dead.** All employers imported. Certifications, education and roles captured through their own lightweight forms. Completed work enters the record within a week of finishing.

**The success criterion that actually matters:** the date the author stops hand-editing the work-summary corpus, because there is no longer a reason to. The test is *"I switched,"* not *"it's finished."*

**Review checkpoint — 2026-11-11.** Not a deadline; nothing fails if M1 is incomplete. It is one scheduled question: *is this still moving?* If yes, continue with no further dates. If no, the response is to cut M1 smaller — not to try harder.

## Explicitly out of scope for v1

1. **Reading work repositories, ticket systems, or git history directly.** The repositories are client-owned and private; the strongest career material left almost no commits anyway. Documents are imported, not harvested.
2. **LinkedIn.** `LATER`, and cheap when it comes — a copy-paste text render off the same facts.
3. **The portfolio site.** A publishing surface with its own design, hosting and audience. Shares the record and nothing else.
4. **The legacy master document.** Retired rather than supported. Maintaining the old system alongside the new one is how migrations die.
5. **Multi-user anything** — accounts, sharing, roles, permissions surfaces.
6. **Consistency checking as a goal.** Drift across documents was deprioritised in session. If the design produces it as a side effect, good; it is not a target.

## The single riskiest assumption

**That a structured fact layer can carry enough nuance to produce documents as good as the ones hand-prompting produces today.**

If facts flatten the material — if the record captures *"reduced latency on a public route"* but loses the reasoning, the false starts and the judgment that make the current case studies compelling — then generated renders will be measurably worse than what exists now, the author will not switch, and the project fails regardless of how well it is engineered.

Everything else is recoverable. This is not.

**How it gets tested:** M1 exists to answer exactly this question, on one employer, before any further investment. The comparison is direct — the generated English résumé against the current hand-produced one. If the generated version is worse, the fix is a richer fact model, not more renders.

**Second-order risk, worth naming:** perfectionism. The project has no deadline and is intended to grow indefinitely, which makes "never actually used" a realistic failure mode. The M1 scope cut and the November checkpoint are the two countermeasures.

## Non-negotiable constraints

- **The existing renders are a fixed contract.** The five documents dictate every field the record layer must carry. 履歴書 in particular follows a conventional format that is not negotiable.
- **Confidentiality is a product requirement, not a footnote.** The corpus mixes the author's own PII, NDA-bound client material, and names of individuals at client and vendor companies. Only the first is the author's to disclose. See `02-product-requirements.md` and the decision log.
- **Nothing the app generates is ever applied without review.** Every regeneration is a proposal, shown as a diff against the current version, accepted or rejected by the author.
