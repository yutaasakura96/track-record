Ledger — real-time freight settlement

Halcyon Freight · Platform Engineering · Mar 2023 – May 2024

Context

Halcyon Freight paid its carriers out of a nightly mainframe batch window that had not changed materially since 2011. Invoices arrived through four intake channels — EDI 210 feeds, the carrier portal, a scanned-document pipeline and plain email — and none of them agreed on what a shipment leg was. Settlement ran once a day, at 01:40, so any dispute raised during business hours waited a full cycle to clear.

I owned the migration of fourteen legacy settlement jobs off that batch window, rebuilding them as event-driven services on the platform team's existing Kafka backbone. Three of the fourteen turned out to be dead code that had been running nightly for years.

What we built

Ledger ingests rate confirmations, proof-of-delivery scans and accessorial charges, normalises them into a single shipment-leg record, and settles that record against the carrier's contracted rate table. The hard part was never the arithmetic; it was agreeing on identity.

I designed the idempotent event-replay layer, which eliminated duplicate payouts by keying every settlement attempt to a deterministic shipment-leg hash. Once replay was provably safe it stopped being an incident manoeuvre and became routine tooling: we replayed the whole of February 2024 twice during the contract-rate migration without a single carrier noticing.

The service is written in Go, with a Postgres ledger of record and one compacted Kafka topic per carrier account. Contract rate tables are versioned and evaluated as data rather than code, which let carrier relations change accessorial rules without waiting on a deploy.

Team and timeline

I led a team of six engineers and two data analysts over fourteen months, reporting to the VP of Platform and working day to day with treasury and carrier relations. We ran a two-week discovery with the mainframe operations group before writing anything, mostly to establish which of the batch jobs were still load-bearing.

Results

Median time from proof-of-delivery to settled invoice fell from 42 hours to 90 seconds, across roughly 1.2 million freight invoices a month. The tail matters more than the median here: the 99th percentile fell from six days to just under four minutes, and that is the number carrier relations quotes in contract talks.

Reconciliation disputes dropped 63% in the two quarters following launch, measured against the same period the previous year and normalised for shipment volume. At the FY24 platform review, Ledger was cited as the year's highest-impact platform project.

Finance never published a consolidated savings figure for the programme. What is documented is that the reconciliation team's manual review queue shrank from about 400 items a day to under 30, and that two contractor seats were not renewed at the end of the year.

Incidents and aftercare

In March 2024 a misconfigured consumer group replayed a partition against a stale offset and paid 1,842 invoices twice, for $310,000 recovered over the following eleven days.

I ran the post-incident review, including the write-up circulated to treasury leadership and the remediation plan that followed. The corrective work — offset fencing, a settlement-attempt ledger and a hard cap on per-run payout volume — shipped over the next six weeks and has held since.

What I would do differently

We treated the carrier portal as a fifth intake channel for far longer than we should have, instead of folding it into the EDI path on day one. It cost us roughly two months of duplicated validation logic and it is still the source of most of the shipment-leg identity edge cases in the backlog.

The second thing is sequencing. We migrated the highest-volume carrier first because the savings were obvious, which meant every early operational surprise landed on the account with the least tolerance for surprises. Starting with a mid-volume regional carrier would have bought us the same learning at a tenth of the blast radius.
